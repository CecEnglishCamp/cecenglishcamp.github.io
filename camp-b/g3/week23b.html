2 Reading · 오늘의 본문
Emma · Jane Austen · 1815
The sun was bright on Box Hill, but the mood turned cold. Emma Woodhouse, bored and restless, decided to entertain her friends with a cruel game. She challenged each person to say "one very clever thing, or two things moderately clever, or three things very dull indeed." When it came to poor, talkative Miss Bates—a kind but simple woman who adored Emma—the moment arrived. With a careless laugh that masked her own discomfort, Emma declared, "You will be limited as to number—only three at most." The words hung in the air. Miss Bates's cheerful face crumpled, though she tried to smile. A deep silence fell. In that instant, Emma saw the raw hurt in the older woman's eyes. The shock of her own cruelty hit her like a physical blow. She had meant to be witty, but she had only been cruel. The laughter died on her lips, replaced by a hot wave of shame that burned deeper than any scolding could. This was no longer a game; it was a revelation of her own careless heart.
B2 · 128 words avg 25.6 w/s
Jane Austen constructs the Box Hill picnic not merely as a social gathering, but as a narrative crucible. Emma, accustomed to being the charming center of her Highbury universe, grows bored. Her restlessness manifests as a desire to orchestrate amusement, leading to the fateful "game" of wit. When her turn comes to engage with Miss Bates—a woman whose genuine kindness and limited intellect make her an easy target for subtle ridicule—Emma's privileged thoughtlessness reaches its peak. She delivers the cutting remark about limiting Miss Bates to "three things very dull indeed," a joke that lands with the weight of a public execution. The immediate aftermath is a masterpiece of emotional silence: Miss Bates's forced smile, the group's uncomfortable stillness, and Emma's own dawning horror. The Box Hill scene, in which Emma unthinkingly humiliated Miss Bates, became a crucial turning point because it forced Emma, through her own shame and Mr. Knightley's subsequent rebuke, to see herself clearly for the first time. This is the moment her self-image as a benevolent queen cracks. The shame is not fleeting embarrassment; it is a profound, moral shock that reconfigures her understanding of her own power and responsibility. Knightley's words later give the shame a name and a lesson, but the initial, visceral experience of causing palpable pain is the true catalyst. It forces her to confront the gap between how she sees herself and the reality of her impact on others, initiating the painful but necessary work of genuine growth.

7 Debate · 디베이트 연습
이번 주 토론 주제
Is the experience of genuine shame truly necessary for meaningful moral growth?
진정한 수치심을 경험해야만 의미 있는 도덕적 성장이 가능한 걸까?
✅ 찬성 입장
Yes. Emma changed only after she felt real shame, because that feeling forced her to see her own behavior.
❌ 반대 입장
No. People can also grow through encouragement, good examples and thinking calmly about their actions.
